A pasta party the night before, a mountain of noodles on your plate – carb loading is as much a part of marathon culture as the race bib. But when does it actually pay off? How much is enough? And do you really need to overhaul your diet for a whole week? Good news: it's far simpler than you think.

Why carbs matter

Your body stores carbohydrates as glycogen, mostly in your muscles and liver. It's your fastest-burning fuel. On long efforts the tank slowly drains – and at some point your legs start to feel like lead[1].

Carb loading simply means filling that tank to the brim in the days before your race. The payoff comes in the second half – exactly when you need it most.

Which races is it worth it for?

Anything that takes longer than about 90 minutes: marathons, long trail runs, cycling marathons, middle- and long-distance triathlons. In races like these, full stores hold off fatigue for noticeably longer – and endurance athletes get to the finish faster[2].

Racing a 10K, or a half marathon you'll finish in under 90 minutes? Skip the special loading. A normal, carb-rich day of eating beforehand does the job[3]. Even an older study of trained runners over 20.9 km found that extra loading made no difference at that distance[4].

How much is enough?

RaceCarbohydrates
under 90 min7–12 g/kg the day before
over 90 min10–12 g/kg a day, for 36–48 hours
Breakfast1–4 g/kg, 1–4 hours before the start

g/kg = grams of carbohydrate per kilo of body weight. Guidelines from the leading sports nutrition bodies[3][1].

Let's do the maths: if you weigh 70 kilos, 10 grams per kilo works out at around 700 grams of carbs a day. That's a lot more than most people expect – which is exactly why a little planning goes a long way.

Two days is all it takes

The old-school advice was to “deplete” first: a week of hard training and hardly any carbs, then a big load at the end. Forget it. Back in the 1980s, researchers showed that your stores fill up just as well without that miserable phase[4].

It can even happen faster than you'd think: trained endurance athletes who took it easy for a single day and ate plenty of carbs had full stores by the next[5]. And since you're training less in race week anyway (more on that in our tapering guide), the two fit together perfectly.

Putting it on your plate

  • Carbs at every meal: pasta, rice, potatoes, bread, oats, fruit.
  • Snack in between: a pretzel, a banana, rice cakes, a cereal bar, a glass of juice.
  • Go easy on fat and fibre: it leaves more room for carbs and keeps your stomach calm. White bread instead of wholemeal is absolutely fine for these two days.
  • Shift, don't stuff: you don't need to eat more overall – just move the carbs to centre stage.
  • Nothing new: stick to foods you know and tolerate well. Test your race fuel on long training runs – your gut can be trained too[6].

A sample day: porridge with banana and honey for breakfast, a pretzel and juice mid-morning, a big bowl of pasta with tomato sauce for lunch, rice cakes and fruit in the afternoon, rice or potatoes for dinner. Plus juice or a sports drink throughout the day.

Don't panic about the scales

Plenty of runners step on the scales on race morning, see an extra kilo or two and panic. Don't – it's a good sign. Your muscles store every gram of glycogen together with around three grams of water[7]. Full tank, mission accomplished.

Race morning

Go for a familiar breakfast two to three hours before the start: white bread with jam or honey, a banana, porridge. Out on the course, gels or a sports drink from the aid stations will keep you going in longer races – our half marathon guide explains how much.

The bottom line

Carb loading isn't rocket science. For races over 90 minutes: two days of plenty of carbs, less training, familiar food – and a good breakfast on race day. You'll start with a full tank and have the energy to carry you all the way to the finish.
