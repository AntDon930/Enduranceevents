The hard work is done, race day is just around the corner – and now you're supposed to train less? That's exactly the trick. In the last week or two, your body catches up on everything you've built in training. Here's what happens and how to get it right.

What is tapering?

To “taper” means to get narrower – like a candle that comes to a point at the top. It describes the last days or weeks before an important race, when you clearly cut back your training. That way you recover from your preparation and arrive at the start line fresh and full of energy[1].

The idea behind it is simple: training makes you fitter – but also tired. Fatigue fades faster than fitness. So if you ease off shortly before the race, the tiredness is gone but the fitness is still there[2].

Does it really work?

Yes – tapering is one of the best-researched topics in endurance sport. Several large reviews of many studies reach the same conclusion: after a good taper, athletes race faster[3][4].

And that's not just true for the pros. Researchers analysed the training data of more than 158,000 recreational marathon runners. Those who cut back their training week by week over the last three weeks finished on average a good five and a half minutes faster than those who trained hard almost until the end[5].

What happens in your body

  • Full energy stores: your muscles top up their glycogen stores – the most important fuel for long distances[2]. Plenty of carbohydrates in the days before the race help with this[7].
  • More red blood cells: your body produces more new blood cells that carry oxygen to your muscles[2].
  • Stronger muscles: the muscle fibres recover and become stronger and faster. In swimmers, this could even be measured directly in individual fibres after three weeks of tapering[6].
  • Better sleep, better mood: many people sleep better, feel better, and the same effort feels easier[2].

In short: you don't just get fitter – you feel it too.

How to get it right

What?How?
Volumecut it roughly in half
Pacekeep it – still run short, brisk sections
Training dayskeep them – don't simply drop runs
Lengthabout two weeks, up to three for a marathon
Progressiona little less step by step, not overnight

These guidelines summarise the large research reviews[3][4].

The most important point: cut the kilometres, not the pace. If you only jog gently during the taper, you often feel sluggish on race day. A few brisk sections keep your legs lively[1].

An example

Let's say you ran 50 kilometres over four days in your biggest week:

  • Two weeks before the race: around 35 kilometres, still over four days. The long run gets shorter, a few kilometres at race pace stay in.
  • Race week: around 20 to 25 kilometres – short, easy runs with a few short strides.
  • The last day or two: very easy or a day off, whatever feels good to you.

For a 10 km race a few days are usually enough; for a marathon it can be longer.

The “taper feeling” is completely normal

Many runners know it: during taper week your legs sometimes feel heavy, you get restless and wonder whether you're losing fitness. Don't worry – you're not. Use the extra time for things that usually get squeezed: go to bed earlier, plan your journey, lay out your race pack and kit, take a look at the course. And then: enjoy the anticipation.

Conclusion

Tapering isn't laziness – it's the final building block of your training. Fewer kilometres, the same rhythm, a little speed – and you'll be on the start line rested, fully fuelled and with fresh legs.
