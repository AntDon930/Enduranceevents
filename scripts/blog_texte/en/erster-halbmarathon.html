21.1 kilometres – that sounds like a lot. But if you already run regularly, your first half marathon is a goal you can reach in a few months. Here's how to prepare, how to enjoy race day and how to recover afterwards.

Are you ready?

A half marathon is exactly half a marathon: 21.0975 kilometres[1]. You're in a good position if you've been running regularly for a few months and can manage 5 to 10 kilometres in one go. Then around three months of preparation is usually enough.

If you haven't done any sport for a while or have health questions, have a quick chat with your doctor first – then you can get started with peace of mind.

The long run is your most important session

The heart of every half marathon preparation is the long run at the weekend. Week by week it gets a little longer – until, a few weeks before the race, you can comfortably run 16 to 18 kilometres. You don't need to run the full distance beforehand; on race day the atmosphere carries you the rest of the way.

What matters is building it up patiently. A large study of more than 5,000 runners shows that problems mainly arise when a single run is suddenly much longer than anything you've run in the past month[2]. By the way, the often-quoted 10 per cent rule for weekly volume isn't as firmly backed by evidence as many think[3] – what matters more is avoiding big jumps.

Rule of thumb: add one or two kilometres to your long run each week and treat yourself to a slightly easier week every three or four weeks. And if something niggles: better take an extra rest day. That way you keep going – and that's what counts.

Mostly easy, sometimes brisk

It sounds strange, but it works: you should run most of your kilometres slowly – slowly enough to hold a conversation. Even elite endurance athletes train easy around 80 per cent of the time and hard only about 20 per cent[4].

For you, that means a week with three to four runs could look like this:

  • two easy runs of 30 to 50 minutes,
  • one brisker run, for example a few kilometres at your planned race pace or short, fast sections,
  • the long, steady run at the weekend.

If you like, add cycling, swimming or some strength work – that strengthens your body without putting extra load on your legs.

The last two weeks: less is more

In the two weeks before the race you clearly cut back your volume, by about half. But keep running on your usual days and add a few short sections at race pace. Studies show that exactly this combination works best[5]. What happens in your body during this time is explained in our article “Tapering: why training less before a race makes you faster”.

Race day

Start calmly

The most common mistake in a first half marathon: setting off too fast because the atmosphere at the start sweeps you along. An even pace usually gets you to the finish best over long distances[6]. Deliberately run the first few kilometres a little slower than you feel like – if you still have something left at the end, all the better.

Eating and drinking

If you'll be out there for more than about an hour, a few carbohydrates on the way help, for example a gel or a cup of sports drink at the aid station. The guideline for longer efforts is 30 to 60 grams per hour[7]. Try out in training what your stomach tolerates – on race day the rule is: nothing new.

Drinking is very simple: drink when you're thirsty. More doesn't help more[8].

Enjoy it

And don't forget to look around: the spectators, the music, the other runners beside you. Your first half marathon only happens once.

After the finish

Give yourself two to three quiet days. Walking or easy cycling feels good; save brisk running for later – one study shows that with rest your muscles recover in about three days[9]. After that you can slowly get back into it. And maybe already look for your next goal.

Conclusion

For your first half marathon you don't need a perfect plan, you need patience: build up the long run slowly, run most kilometres easy, rest before the race, start calmly and drink to thirst. The atmosphere along the course and the feeling at the finish take care of the rest.
