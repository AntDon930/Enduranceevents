You want to do your first triathlon but don't know where to start? Here you'll find out which distance suits you, how to spot a beginner-friendly race and how to get to the start line well prepared in twelve weeks.

1. Which distance suits you?

Not all triathlons are the same. The formats differ mainly in length – and therefore in how much training you need. The figures come from the competition rules of the German Triathlon Union (DTU), which are based on the distances of the world governing body World Triathlon[1][2]. The federations set ranges; many organisers fall somewhere within them.

FormatSwimBikeRunFor whom?

Sprint (often called “Volks” or “Jedermann” distance)500–750 m18–22 km4.5–5.5 kmThe classic way in

Middle distance (70.3)1.9–2.9 km80–90 km18–22 kmAfter a season or two
Long distance (140.6)3–4 km91–200 km22–42.2 kmThe big goal for later

The Ironman formats 70.3 and 140.6 are named after their total length in miles: 1.9 + 90 + 21.1 km and 3.8 + 180 + 42.2 km.

Our recommendation: start with a sprint or super sprint – that's what the DTU advises beginners too[3]. There you learn the essentials – the transition zone, the feeling at the start, the switch from bike to run – without the distance overwhelming you. A rule of thumb from experience: if you can swim 500 metres in one go, cycle for an hour and run for 30 minutes without stopping, you have a good foundation for a sprint.

2. How to spot a beginner-friendly race

  • Swimming in a pool or a calm lake: a pool triathlon with staggered starts is the most relaxed way in. At a mass start in a lake, it helps to line up at the side or at the back.
  • Flat bike course and a loop: few metres of climbing and a closed-off loop take a lot of stress out of it.
  • A “Jedermann” or beginners' category: then you start with like-minded people rather than the club athletes.
  • Enough lead time: depending on your basic fitness, the DTU suggests three to six months of preparation[3].
  • A short journey: a race nearby saves you an overnight stay and lets you ride the course beforehand.

Tip: read the organiser's race information carefully. It tells you whether a wetsuit is allowed or compulsory, whether drafting is banned and which bikes are permitted. A road bike isn't compulsory: for non-drafting races the DTU doesn't prescribe a type of bike, but it does require, among other things, two independent brakes and plugged handlebar ends[1]. The DTU itself encourages beginners to race on the bike they already have[3].

4. Twelve weeks to your first start

Twelve weeks are enough if you already have some basic fitness – the DTU offers free training plans for the sprint distance over 12 and 20 weeks[4]. Our rough framework is based on experience: about four to six sessions a week of 30 to 60 minutes.

Weeks 1–4: the basics

  • Two sessions each of swimming, cycling and running, all at an easy pace.
  • Work on your swimming technique – a technique session or a club session pays off most here.

Weeks 5–9: building up

  • Slowly lengthen your sessions, and go a bit faster once a week.
  • Brick training: run for 10–20 minutes straight after cycling. That gets your legs used to the switch.
  • If possible, swim in open water at least twice, in a wetsuit if the water is cool.
  • Practise the transition at home: helmet on, shoes on, turn your race number round.

Weeks 10–12: fine-tuning

  • In the second-to-last week, do half the race distance in one go.
  • In the final week, clearly cut back your volume and go to the start well rested.

If you haven't done any sport for a while, get some brief medical advice before you start training – the DTU recommends this too[3]. Then you can get going with peace of mind.

5. Rules beginners often don't know

  • Helmet first: your helmet must be fastened before you take your bike off the rack in the transition zone, and it stays fastened until the bike is back on the rack[1][2].
  • Mounting and dismounting only beyond or before the marked line at the exit of the transition zone – no riding inside the transition zone[1].
  • Race number: at the back on the bike, at the front on the run, not at all in the swim[1]. With a race belt you can turn it round in a second.
  • No music: devices that distract from the race – music is explicitly included – are banned, and breaking the rule leads to disqualification[1].
  • Drafting: in age-group and “Jedermann” races drafting is usually banned. The drafting zone is 12 metres long, and an overtaking manoeuvre must be completed within 25 seconds[1].

Wetsuit: what the water temperature decides

Whether you may or must wear a wetsuit is set out in the DTU competition rules according to water temperature and swim distance[1]. For age groups up to 59 years:

Swim distanceCompulsoryAllowedBanned
up to 1,500 m14.0–15.9 °C16.0–21.9 °Cfrom 22.0 °C
over 1,500 m14.0–15.9 °C16.0–24.5 °Cfrom 24.6 °C

From the 60+ age group, the wetsuit is allowed on all distances up to 24.5 °C. Below 14 °C there is no swim at all according to the rules[1]. The organiser will tell you the temperature measured on race day before the start.

6. Race-day checklist

  • Race pack, race number and race belt
  • Swim cap and goggles – if the organiser provides a cap, you must wear it[1]
  • Wetsuit, if allowed or compulsory (see above)
  • Bike, helmet, cycling shoes or trainers, water bottle
  • Running shoes – elastic laces save time
  • A towel for your transition spot, sun cream, something to eat afterwards

Arrive early: the check-in times for the transition zone are in the race information and are binding – if you're late, you may lose your start[1]. At check-in you wear your helmet on your head, fastened[1]. Walk the routes from “swim in”, “bike out” and “run out” once and remember where your spot is – after the swim everything looks different.

Conclusion

Your first triathlon isn't rocket science: a short distance, a race nearby, twelve weeks of preparation – and a little courage at the start line. The goal the first time is simply to reach the finish.
