Clear water, endless views, no tumble turns at the wall: open water swimming is a whole different world from the pool – and for many swimmers, it's the most beautiful way to swim there is. Here's how to dive into your first open water event fully prepared.

What makes open water different

No wall to push off, no lane ropes, usually cooler water and a crowd at the start – it all feels new at first, but you'll get used to it fast once you know what's coming.

The Swiss Life Saving Society (SLRG) puts it in a nutshell: open water – rivers, ponds and lakes – is for good, practised swimmers[7]. At an organised event you're in safe hands, with lifeguards, boats and a marked course. Add the golden rules of the German Life Saving Association (DLRG)[3]:

  • Only swim where someone can help if needed – and let someone know you're going in.
  • Cool off before you get in.
  • Only swim when you feel good.
  • Lightning, thunder or heavy rain? Get out of the water straight away.

Those first minutes in cool water

If you've ever stepped into cold water, you know the feeling: you gasp, your breathing speeds up, your heart races. It's called cold shock – a completely normal reaction that peaks in the first 30 seconds and fades within two minutes[1].

The UK Met Office, together with the RNLI and RLSS UK, says cold shock can kick in below 15 °C, with the first effects lasting around 90 seconds. Their advice: stay calm, lean back and float, head above water[2].

What that means for you: wade in slowly instead of jumping, take your first strokes nice and easy, and give your breathing a moment to settle. Out of breath? Roll onto your back, breathe, then carry on. And the more often you swim in cool water, the less you'll notice it – your body gets used to conditions it knows[1].

Wetsuits: when they're a must – and when they're banned

A wetsuit keeps you warm and helps you float. Whether you can – or must – wear one depends on the water temperature and on which rules the event follows.

World Aquatics takes the temperature two hours before the start, at three points on the course and 40 centimetres below the surface[4]. The German Triathlon Union has extra rules, for example for over-60s and on cold days[5].

Many lake crossings and open swims set their own rules – some even run separate categories with and without wetsuits. So check the event details. And if you're planning to wear one, test it in open water first: a suit that's too tight can feel suffocating, especially in the first few minutes.

The start – and staying on course

The start is the most exciting moment: lots of swimmers, not much space. That's why good organisers send swimmers off in waves and set out big, bright buoys[1]. For you, that means:

  • Start at the side or the back: find your rhythm without elbows and feet in your face.
  • Study the course: memorise the buoys and a few landmarks on the shore – a church tower, a house, a big tree.
  • Sight regularly: every few strokes, lift your head for a quick look at the next buoy. Practise it in the pool first.

Training in open water: the tow float

When training outside of events, many swimmers tow an inflatable float behind them. Its biggest plus, according to the DLRG, is visibility – to boats, surfers and other swimmers. But it's not a rescue device, especially not in rivers and canals[6]. Training is more fun with a buddy or a group anyway – ideally close to the shore.

Going with the flow: river swimming

River swimming has a magic all of its own – in Basel, a swim in the Rhine is practically a local tradition. The canton of Basel-Stadt has clear advice: big ships can't swerve around swimmers, and their captains can't see much. So steer clear of the shipping lanes, and give moored boats, buoys and bridge piers a wide berth[8]. The SLRG adds: the colder the water, the shorter your swim, because getting chilled can bring on muscle cramps[7].

At an organised river swim, the course, the entry and exit points and the safety cover are all taken care of. On your own, only swim in a river where it's explicitly allowed and where you know the spot.

How clean is the water?

Good news: in the 2025 bathing season, 2,291 German bathing sites were tested. 98 per cent met the standards of the EU Bathing Water Directive – and 91 per cent were rated “excellent”. More than half of the temporary swimming bans were down to cyanobacteria, better known as blue-green algae[9]. Up-to-date results are published by the German states, on the bathing water map linked by the German Environment Agency, and for all of Europe by the European Environment Agency[9][10].

For blue-green algae, the German Environment Agency has a simple test: stand knee-deep in the water. If you can't see your feet through a green-blue haze, give swimming a miss that day[11].

After the swim: wrap up warm

Getting out isn't quite the end of it. You can keep cooling down for a while after you've left the water, and your circulation needs a moment to adjust to dry land[1]. So take your time standing up, dry off, wrap up warm and grab a hot drink. Feeling dizzy? Sit down for a minute.

Your open water checklist

  • Read the event details: course, water temperature, wetsuit rules, cut-off time
  • Get a few open water sessions in beforehand – never alone
  • Test your wetsuit (if allowed) in open water before race day
  • A bright swim cap and goggles – plus a spare pair in your bag
  • Memorise the buoys and landmarks before the start
  • Wade in slowly and start at the side or the back
  • Out of breath? Roll onto your back and breathe
  • Warm clothes and a hot drink for afterwards

The bottom line

Open water swimming is one of the most beautiful experiences in endurance sport – once you know its quirks: those first minutes in cool water, the wetsuit rules, the scrum at the start and the rules of the river. An organised event with lifeguards is the perfect way in. So pick one and dive in. Slowly, of course.
