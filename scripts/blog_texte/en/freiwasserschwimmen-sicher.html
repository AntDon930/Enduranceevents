Clear water, a wide view, no turning at the wall: open water swimming feels completely different from the pool – and for many people it's the most beautiful way to swim. Here's how to start your first open water event well prepared.

What's different in open water

No pool edge, no lane ropes, often cooler water and lots of people at the start – you get used to all of this quickly once you know what to expect.

The Swiss Life Saving Society (SLRG) sums it up in its basic rule: only good, practised swimmers should venture into open water – rivers, ponds and lakes[7]. At an organised event you're in very good hands: lifeguards, boats and marked courses are all part of it. On top of that come the basic rules of the German Life Saving Association (DLRG)[3]:

  • Only go into the water if someone can help if there are problems – and tell someone that you're going swimming.
  • Cool down before going in.
  • Only swim if you feel well.
  • In case of lightning, thunder or heavy rain, get out of the water immediately.

The first minutes in cool water

Anyone who has gone into cool water knows the feeling: you gasp for air, breathe fast, your heart pounds. Experts call this cold shock. The reaction is completely normal, peaks in the first 30 seconds and subsides within the first two minutes[1].

The UK Met Office, together with the rescue organisations RNLI and RLSS UK, gives water below 15 °C as the range in which cold shock can occur. The first effects last about 90 seconds. The advice: stay calm, lean back and float, keeping your head above water[2].

What this means for you: walk into the water slowly rather than jumping in, take your first strokes calmly and give your breathing time to settle. If you run out of breath: briefly turn onto your back, breathe, then carry on. And the more often you swim in cool water, the less you feel the effect – your body adapts to the conditions it knows[1].

Wetsuits: when they're compulsory and when they're banned

A wetsuit keeps you warm and gives you buoyancy. Whether you may or must wear one depends on the water temperature – and on the rules the event follows.

World Aquatics measures two hours before the start at three points on the course, at a depth of 40 centimetres[4]. The German Triathlon Union has additional rules, for example for age groups from 60 and for cold air[5].

Many lake crossings and open swims set their own rules – some have separate categories with and without wetsuits. So check the event information. And if you want to wear a wetsuit: try it out in open water beforehand. A suit that's too tight can feel constricting, especially in the first few minutes.

Start and navigation

At the start, lots of people swim in a small space – that's the most exciting moment. Good organisers therefore start in waves and set out clearly visible buoys[1]. For you, that means:

  • Start at the side or the back: that way you can settle in without arms and feet in your face.
  • Look at the course beforehand: memorise the buoys and striking landmarks on the shore – a church tower, a house, a tree.
  • Look ahead regularly: lift your head briefly every few strokes to sight the next buoy. Practise this in the pool.

Training in open water: the tow float

For training outside events, many swimmers use an inflatable tow float. According to the DLRG, its biggest advantage is better visibility – for boats, surfers and other swimmers. But it isn't a rescue device, especially not in rivers and canals[6]. Training is most fun in pairs or as a group anyway – and ideally close to the shore.

Swimming in a river

River swimming has a charm all of its own – in Basel, for example, swimming in the Rhine is a tradition. The canton of Basel-Stadt gives clear advice for it: large ships cannot get out of swimmers' way, and the captains' view is limited. The recommendation is not to swim in the shipping lanes. Give moored boats, buoys and bridge piers a wide berth[8]. The SLRG adds: the colder the water, the shorter your swim should be, because getting too cold can lead to muscle cramps[7].

At an organised river swim, the course, entry and exit points and safety cover are all fixed. On your own, only use a river where it is expressly permitted and where you know the spot.

Water quality and blue-green algae

The good news: in the 2025 bathing season the authorities tested 2,291 German bathing waters. 98 per cent met the requirements of the EU Bathing Water Directive, and 91 per cent were even rated “excellent”. More than half of the temporary bathing bans were due to cyanobacteria (“blue-green algae”)[9]. You can find current results from the federal states, via the bathing water map the German Environment Agency refers to, and for the whole of Europe from the European Environment Agency[9][10].

For blue-green algae, the German Environment Agency gives a simple rule of thumb: if you're standing knee-deep in the water and can no longer see your feet because of green-blue cloudiness, it's better not to swim[11].

After your swim: wrap up warm

It isn't all over once you're out of the water. After swimming you can keep getting colder for a while – your body continues to cool down even when you're already out. And your circulation needs a moment to get used to being on land[1]. So stand up slowly, dry yourself off, get dressed warmly and have something warm to drink. If you feel dizzy, sit down for a moment.

Checklist for your first open water event

  • Read the event information: course, water temperature, wetsuit rule, time limit
  • Train in open water at least a few times beforehand – never alone
  • Test your wetsuit (if allowed) in open water beforehand
  • A brightly coloured swim cap and goggles, with a spare pair in your bag
  • Memorise the buoys and landmarks before the start
  • Walk into the water slowly, start at the side or the back
  • If you run out of breath: turn onto your back and breathe
  • Warm clothes and a warm drink for afterwards

Conclusion

Open water swimming is one of the most beautiful endurance experiences there is – as long as you know what makes it special: the first minutes in cool water, the wetsuit rules, the bustle at the start and the rules in rivers. An organised event with water rescue cover is the perfect way in – pick one and jump in. Slowly, of course.
