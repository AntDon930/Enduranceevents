RTF, cycling marathon, “Jedermann” race or Gran Fondo: which cycling event suits you?

RTF, cycling marathon, “Jedermann” race, Gran Fondo – in cycling, events go by very different names, and not every one of them is a race. Here you'll find out what's behind the terms, which rules apply and which format suits your first organised cycling event.

Two worlds: tour or race

The most important question first: do you want to finish, or ride against the clock? Cycling events fall into two groups:

  • Recreational tours such as RTF, CTF, cycling marathons and brevets: riding together, with food stations, but without timing and without rankings.
  • Races such as “Jedermann” races and Gran Fondos: with timing, results lists and age groups.

The rules in this article come directly from the federations – from the German cycling federation (now “German Cycling”, formerly BDR)[1], from Cycling Austria and from the world governing body UCI.

RTF: the relaxed way in

An RTF is an organised ride offered by a cycling club on a fixed date. You choose between several routes – from 20 to 199 kilometres – and along the way there are food stations with drinks and snacks[1]. That's also where you briefly confirm that you've passed through – with a stamp or digitally[3].

What else you should know[1]:

  • No clock: timing is explicitly forbidden. Nobody measures how fast you are – you ride at your own pace.
  • Public roads: the road traffic regulations apply. That includes traffic lights, right of way and keeping to the right.
  • Helmets compulsory: all RTF participants must wear a helmet – unlike in normal road traffic, where there is no legal helmet requirement for cyclists in Germany[9].
  • Route: usually signposted. Some organisers guide you fully or partly by GPS track; that's stated in the recreational cycling calendar.
  • E-bikes are allowed as long as the motor assistance stops at 25 km/h.
  • Age: children and young people up to 14 only ride with an accompanying adult; young people pay no entry fee until their 18th birthday.
  • Rankings: if you want to collect the kilometres you ride, you need a recreational cycling licence. You can ride along without one.

For gravel and mountain bike fans the same exists off-road: a CTF takes you along farm tracks and forest paths, with routes from 15 to 99 kilometres, food stations, compulsory helmets and, again, no timing[2].

Cycling marathons and brevets: when it gets long

As the federation defines them, cycling marathons are road tours of 200 kilometres or more[1]. Routes over 200 kilometres may only be ridden from the age of 18. If you like even more adventure, ride a brevet: the route isn't signposted, you navigate with a GPS track and look after yourself. The usual distances are 200, 300, 400, 600 and 1,000 kilometres[1].

“Jedermann” races: your first real race

A “Jedermann” (everyone) race is a race for riders without an annual licence. It often runs as a support race within a larger cycling event[4]. Under the competition rules you start with a day licence, timing is used to produce rankings for women, men and age groups, and prize money is not allowed[4].

Important: a “Jedermann” race doesn't automatically take place on closed roads. The rules require participants to obey the road traffic regulations at all times, and they prescribe marshals at junctions as well as lead and rear vehicles[4]. Whether the course is closed is stated in the event information. Races on public roads need a permit from the authorities[8].

In Austria, Cycling Austria calls these events “Cycling for All”. The rules there include: the minimum age is the junior category, helmets are compulsory, the road traffic rules must be obeyed at all times, and music and headphones are banned during the event[6].

Gran Fondo: a race with a World Championships ticket

“Gran Fondo” is Italian for “great distance”. In the UCI Gran Fondo World Series the term is precisely defined: a series for amateurs with a mass start, timing over the whole course and road races between 80 and 225 kilometres[5]. Results are by age group, and the best part: if you finish in the fastest quarter of your age group, you can race at the Gran Fondo World Championships. A helmet is compulsory. The UCI recommends a full road closure but doesn't require it[5].

Outside this series, many organisers use the name more freely, for example for big “Jedermann” tours. Here too: the event information tells you whether there is timing, a road closure and a licence requirement.

Riding as a group on the road: what German traffic law allows

At RTFs and many “Jedermann” events you ride in normal traffic. You should know three rules from the German road traffic regulations (StVO; quotes in our translation):

  • Keep right: “Ride as far to the right as possible”[7].
  • Side by side: “Cyclists may ride side by side if this does not obstruct traffic; otherwise they must ride in single file.”[7]
  • Closed formation: more than 15 cyclists may form a closed group and then ride two abreast on the road. The group must be clearly recognisable as such to others; long groups must leave gaps for other traffic[10].

Some clubs offer RTFs in a closed group: road captains set the pace, the stated average speed is capped, and a sweeper rider accompanies the group[1]. That's ideal for getting started if you don't have much experience of riding in a group yet.

Eating and drinking on long routes

On a long tour, fuelling helps decide how well you get to the finish:

  • Carbohydrates: for longer efforts, 30 to 60 grams per hour is a sensible target; for efforts over two and a half hours, up to 90 grams per hour can help[11]. Amounts that high only work with a mix of different sugars, such as glucose and fructose[12].
  • Pace matters: the recommendations depend on intensity. If you're riding at a leisurely pace, you don't necessarily need the maximum amounts that apply to competitive athletes[12]. Try out in training what works for you.
  • Drinking: the aim is to avoid losing more than 2 per cent of your body weight in fluid. You can find out how much you sweat by weighing yourself before and after a longer ride[13].

At an RTF the food stations take care of part of this for you. On a brevet you're responsible yourself – plan petrol stations, bakeries and water fountains in advance.

Which format suits you?

  • You just want to ride a nice, organised loop: an RTF of 50 to 100 kilometres, ideally in a closed group.
  • You prefer riding off-road: a CTF on your gravel bike or mountain bike.
  • You're looking for a big challenge without a stopwatch: a cycling marathon of over 200 kilometres or a brevet.
  • You want to know how you compare: a “Jedermann” race or a Gran Fondo with age-group rankings.

This is our own assessment, not a rule from the federations. For every format: read the event information before you sign up – it lists route lengths, start times, licence rules and whether the roads are closed.

Conclusion

For getting started, an RTF is almost always the best choice: no pressure, good food stations, a signposted route and lots of like-minded riders. If you want more afterwards, step up to a cycling marathon – or try a “Jedermann” race to find out what a real race feels like.
