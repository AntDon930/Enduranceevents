Ride or race? Find the cycling event that's right for you

RTF, cycling marathon, “Jedermann” race, Gran Fondo – cycling events go by a confusing mix of names, and not all of them are actually races. Here's what each one really means, which rules apply, and which format is the perfect fit for your first organised ride.

Ride or race?

First, the big question: do you want to enjoy the ride, or race against the clock? Cycling events fall into two camps:

  • Sportives and tours like the RTF, CTF, cycling marathons and brevets: you ride together, there's food along the way – but no timing and no rankings.
  • Races like “Jedermann” races and Gran Fondos: timing chips, results lists and age groups.

The rules in this article come straight from the federations: German Cycling (the German federation, formerly BDR)[1], Cycling Austria and the world governing body, the UCI.

RTF: the easy way in

An RTF (short for Radtourenfahrt) is an organised ride run by a cycling club on a set date – think of it as a sportive without the stopwatch. You pick from several routes, anywhere from 20 to 199 kilometres, and there are feed stations with drinks and snacks along the way[1]. That's also where you get checked through – with a stamp or digitally[3].

Good to know[1]:

  • No clock: timing is strictly off-limits. Nobody tracks your speed – you ride at your own pace.
  • Open roads: normal traffic rules apply – red lights, right of way and keeping right included.
  • Helmets on: every RTF rider has to wear one – even though there's no legal helmet requirement for cyclists in everyday German traffic[9].
  • Route: usually signposted. Some organisers send you out with a GPS track for all or part of the way; the event calendar tells you which.
  • E-bikes are welcome, as long as the motor stops assisting at 25 km/h.
  • Age: kids up to 14 ride with an accompanying adult, and under-18s ride for free.
  • Points and rankings: if you want to collect kilometres in the season standings, you'll need a recreational licence. Without one, you can still ride.

Gravel or mountain bike fan? There's an off-road version too: the CTF takes you along farm tracks and forest trails, with routes from 15 to 99 kilometres, feed stations, compulsory helmets – and once again, no timing[2].

Cycling marathons and brevets: going long

By the federation's definition, a cycling marathon is a tour of 200 kilometres or more[1] – and you have to be 18 to ride anything over 200. Craving even more adventure? Try a brevet: no signs, no feed stations – just you, a GPS track and whatever you can find along the way. Classic distances are 200, 300, 400, 600 and 1,000 kilometres[1].

“Jedermann” races: your first taste of racing

“Jedermann” means “everyone” – and that's the point: these races are open to riders without an annual racing licence. They often run as a support race at a bigger cycling event[4]. You race on a day licence, timing produces rankings for women, men and age groups, and there's no prize money[4].

One thing to know: a “Jedermann” race doesn't automatically mean closed roads. The rules say riders must obey traffic laws at all times, with marshals at junctions plus lead and sweep vehicles[4]. Whether the course is closed is in the event details. Any race on public roads needs an official permit[8].

In Austria, Cycling Austria calls these events “Cycling for All”. The rules there: you have to be at least junior age, helmets are compulsory, traffic rules apply throughout, and music and headphones are banned during the event[6].

Gran Fondo: your ticket to the Worlds

“Gran Fondo” is Italian for “big distance”. In the UCI Gran Fondo World Series the term has a precise meaning: amateur road races of 80 to 225 kilometres, with a mass start and timing from start to finish[5]. Results are by age group – and here's the exciting part: finish in the fastest quarter of your age group and you qualify for the Gran Fondo World Championships. Helmets are compulsory, and the UCI recommends – but doesn't require – fully closed roads[5].

Outside that series, many organisers use the name more loosely, for example for big mass-participation rides. Again, the event details tell you whether there's timing, closed roads or a licence requirement.

Riding in a group: what German traffic law says

At RTFs and many “Jedermann” events you share the road with normal traffic. Three rules from the German Highway Code (StVO, quoted in our translation) are worth knowing:

  • Keep right: “Ride as far to the right as possible”[7].
  • Side by side: “Cyclists may ride side by side if this does not obstruct traffic; otherwise they must ride in single file.”[7]
  • Riding as a bunch: groups of more than 15 cyclists can ride as a closed formation, two abreast on the road. The group has to be clearly recognisable as one, and long groups must leave gaps for other traffic[10].

Some clubs run their RTFs as a closed group: road captains set the pace, the average speed has a cap, and a sweeper rides at the back[1]. Perfect if you're new to riding in a bunch.

Fuelling for the long haul

On a long ride, what you eat and drink goes a long way towards how you feel at the finish:

  • Carbs: on longer rides, aim for 30 to 60 grams an hour; beyond two and a half hours, up to 90 grams an hour can help[11]. Amounts that high only work with a mix of sugars, such as glucose and fructose[12].
  • Match it to your pace: the guidelines depend on intensity. Cruising along at a relaxed pace, you won't necessarily need the maximum amounts recommended for competitive athletes[12]. Use your training rides to find what works.
  • Drink smart: try not to lose more than 2 per cent of your body weight in fluid. Weigh yourself before and after a long ride to find out how much you sweat[13].

On an RTF, the feed stations do a lot of the work for you. On a brevet it's all on you – plan your stops at petrol stations, bakeries and water fountains in advance.

Which one is for you?

  • You want a great day out on a well-organised route: an RTF of 50 to 100 kilometres, ideally in a closed group.
  • You'd rather be off-road: a CTF on your gravel or mountain bike.
  • You want an epic challenge without the stopwatch: a 200-plus-kilometre cycling marathon or a brevet.
  • You want to see how you stack up: a “Jedermann” race or a Gran Fondo with age-group rankings.

That's our take, not an official rule. Whatever you choose, read the event details before you sign up – route lengths, start times, licence rules and road closures are all in there.

The bottom line

For your first event, an RTF is almost always the way to go: no pressure, great feed stations, a signposted route and plenty of like-minded riders. Hungry for more? Step up to a cycling marathon – or line up for a “Jedermann” race and find out what real racing feels like.
