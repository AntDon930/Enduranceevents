Your first half marathon: from “Can I really do this?” to “I'm signing up for a marathon”

21.1 kilometres sounds like a long way. But if you're already running regularly, your first half marathon is well within reach – a few months from now. Here's how to train for it, how to soak up race day and how to recover afterwards.

Are you ready?

A half marathon is exactly half a marathon: 21.0975 kilometres[1]. You're in a great starting position if you've been running regularly for a few months and can manage 5 to 10 kilometres in one go. From there, around three months of preparation is usually plenty.

If you haven't done much sport for a while or have any health questions, have a quick word with your doctor first – then you can get going with total peace of mind.

The long run is your secret weapon

The heart of any half marathon plan is the weekend long run. Week by week, it gets a little longer – until, a few weeks before race day, you can comfortably cover 16 to 18 kilometres. You don't need to run the full distance in training. On the day, the crowds will carry you the rest of the way.

The key is patience. A large study of more than 5,000 runners found that trouble mostly starts when a single run is suddenly much longer than anything you've done in the past month[2]. The famous 10 per cent rule for weekly mileage, by the way, isn't as solidly backed by evidence as many believe[3] – what really matters is avoiding big jumps.

Rule of thumb: add one or two kilometres to your long run each week, and give yourself a lighter week every three or four weeks. If something starts to niggle, take an extra rest day. Consistency beats heroics – every time.

Mostly easy, sometimes fast

It sounds counterintuitive, but it works: most of your kilometres should be slow – slow enough to chat while you run. Even elite endurance athletes spend about 80 per cent of their training at an easy effort and only around 20 per cent going hard[4].

So a week with three or four runs could look like this:

  • two easy runs of 30 to 50 minutes,
  • one quicker session – a few kilometres at your goal race pace, or short, fast intervals,
  • the long, steady run at the weekend.

Fancy more? Add some cycling, swimming or strength work – it builds your engine without putting extra miles on your legs.

The final two weeks: less is more

In the last two weeks before the race, cut your volume right back – by about half. Keep running on your usual days, though, and sprinkle in a few short stretches at race pace. Studies show this combination works best[5]. What happens in your body during that time is explained in “Tapering: train less, race faster”.

Race day

Start slow

The number one first-timer mistake: shooting off too fast because the buzz at the start sweeps you along. Over long distances, an even pace usually gets you to the finish in the best shape[6]. Run the first few kilometres a little slower than you feel like running – if you've still got something left at the end, even better.

Fuel and fluids

If you'll be out there for more than an hour or so, a few carbs on the way will help – a gel, or a cup of sports drink at an aid station. For longer efforts, the guideline is 30 to 60 grams of carbohydrate per hour[7]. Use your training runs to find out what your stomach can handle. On race day, the golden rule is: nothing new.

Drinking couldn't be simpler: drink when you're thirsty. More doesn't help[8].

Enjoy every moment

And don't forget to look around: the crowds, the music, the runners beside you. You only get one first half marathon.

After the finish line

Give yourself two or three easy days. A walk or a gentle bike ride will do you good; save the faster running for later – one study found that with rest, your muscles recover in about three days[9]. Then ease back into training. And maybe start hunting for your next goal.

The bottom line

Your first half marathon doesn't need a perfect plan – it needs patience. Build up your long run gradually, keep most of your running easy, rest before the race, start slow and drink when you're thirsty. The atmosphere and the feeling at the finish will take care of the rest.
