Carb loading: what to eat in the days before your race

A pasta party the night before, a plate piled high with noodles – carb loading belongs to the marathon like the race bib. But when is it really worth it, how much is enough, and do you have to change your diet for a whole week? The good news: it's simpler than many people think.

What it's about

Your body stores carbohydrates as glycogen – mainly in your muscles and your liver. This store is your fastest fuel. During long efforts it gradually empties, and at some point your legs start to feel heavy[1].

Carb loading simply means filling this store to the brim in the days before the race. Then you have more energy for the second half – exactly where it counts.

Which races is it worth it for?

For anything that lasts longer than about 90 minutes: marathons, long trail runs, cycling marathons, middle and long-distance triathlons. In races like these, full stores keep fatigue away noticeably longer, and endurance athletes reach the finish faster[2].

For a 10 km race or a half marathon you finish in under 90 minutes, you don't need any special carb loading. Eating normally with plenty of carbohydrates the day before is enough[3]. An older study of trained runners over 20.9 km already showed that extra loading brought no benefit over that distance[4].

How much is enough?

RaceCarbohydrates
under 90 min7–12 g/kg the day before
over 90 min10–12 g/kg per day, for 36–48 hours
Breakfast1–4 g/kg, 1–4 hours before the start

g/kg = grams of carbohydrate per kilo of body weight. Guideline values from the major sports nutrition bodies[3][1].

A quick calculation: if you weigh 70 kilos, 10 grams per kilo means around 700 grams of carbohydrates a day. That's more than most people expect – which is exactly why a little planning pays off.

Two days are enough

It used to be said that you should first “train yourself empty” for a week and eat hardly any carbohydrates, then load up all the more. You don't need that. As early as the 1980s it turned out that the stores fill up just as well without this tough phase[4].

And it works even faster than you might think: trained endurance athletes who trained little for one day and ate plenty of carbohydrates already had full stores afterwards[5]. Since you train less in race week anyway (more on that in our article on tapering), the two fit together perfectly.

How to put it into practice

  • Carbohydrates in every meal: pasta, rice, potatoes, bread, oats, fruit.
  • Snack in between: a pretzel, a banana, rice cakes, cereal bars, a glass of juice.
  • A little less fat and fibre: that leaves more room for carbohydrates on your plate and keeps your stomach calm. White bread instead of wholemeal is perfectly fine for these two days.
  • Not “eat more” but “distribute differently”: you don't have to stuff yourself – you just shift the focus on your plate.
  • Nothing new: eat things you know and tolerate well. Test your race nutrition on long training runs – your stomach can get used to it[6].

A sample day: oats with banana and honey for breakfast, a pretzel and juice mid-morning, a big plate of pasta with tomato sauce for lunch, rice cakes and fruit in the afternoon, rice or potatoes in the evening. Plus juice or a sports drink spread over the day.

Don't worry about the scales

Many people are one or two kilos heavier on race day and get a fright. That's a good sign: your muscles store every gram of glycogen together with around three grams of water[7]. Your stores are full – exactly what you wanted.

On race day itself

A familiar breakfast two to three hours before the start: white bread with jam or honey, a banana, oats. And on the course, gels or a sports drink at the aid stations help during long races – how much is explained in our half marathon guide.

Conclusion

Carb loading isn't rocket science: for races over 90 minutes, two days of plenty of carbohydrates, less training, familiar foods – and a good breakfast on race day. Then you'll be on the start line with full stores and the energy to carry you to the finish.
